# 3. Research the Operators, Not the Spectators

**Learn from people who actually do the thing. Primary sources, real numbers, labeled assumptions.**

## The problem this solves

Most easily-found "research" is spectators talking to spectators. Surface-level forum threads, recycled listicles, and confident strangers who have never actually done the thing. It feels like research because it's text on a screen with opinions in it. It teaches you almost nothing about what actually works, because none of the people writing it know.

## The rule

Go find where the people who **actually succeed at this** talk, and learn from them.

- Operators, not commentators. People with results, not people with takes.
- Primary sources: real numbers, named people, named companies, case studies with outcomes attached.
- Paid-tool data, industry reports, and public post-mortems over generic blog summaries.
- When you cite something, say where it came from. Every time.

## A quick quality test

Ask of any source: *does this person have skin in the game, and can I see their results?*

| Weak signal | Strong signal |
|---|---|
| Anonymous opinion, no outcome | Named operator with a track record |
| "Everyone knows..." | A number, with a source |
| A summary of a summary | The primary source itself |
| General advice that fits anything | Specifics that only apply to this exact situation |

## Standards

- Barebones research is a failure, not a starting point. If it took five minutes, it's not done.
- Never present an assumption as a finding. If you're inferring, say "I'm inferring."
- State confidence and gaps explicitly. "High confidence on X, thin on Y" is a complete, honest answer.
- A wall is not a stopping point. If the obvious source is blocked, find another route to the same fact (see [Beat the Roadblock](06-beat-the-roadblock.md)).

## The honest version

Good research is slow and a little uncomfortable, because it keeps disproving things you wanted to be true. That discomfort is the signal that it's working. The goal isn't to feel informed. It's to be right about something specific enough to bet on.

## Checklist

- [ ] Did I find people with actual results, not just opinions?
- [ ] Do I have real numbers and named sources, not vibes?
- [ ] Did I label what's a finding vs. what's an assumption?
- [ ] Did I state my confidence and the gaps?
